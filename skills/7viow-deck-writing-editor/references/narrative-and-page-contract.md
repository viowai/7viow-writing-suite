# Narrative and Page Writing

Use for architecture, page writing, sequence changes, or capacity decisions.

## Audience movement

Describe where the audience starts, the question they need answered, the evidence that changes their understanding, and the decision or belief the deck can support. Work within the known communication purpose: the author's position, the audience's interests, and any intended commercial action. A proposal should make the supported case for its offer; an evaluation should serve its stated decision. Preserve the facts and necessary conditions in either form.

Use the shortest coherent sequence that establishes the understandings the audience needs. A necessary distinction, comparison, mechanism, or implication earns its place before the conclusion that depends on it. Brevity follows coherence; a conventional chapter list does not establish it.

For each section or substantive page, determine:

1. What question arises at this point?
2. What does the audience already know?
3. What distinct understanding does this page add?
4. Why does it belong here, and what does it make possible next?

A page's primary proposition may have several supporting facts. A new page is justified by a new audience job, a necessary transition, or capacity; rephrasing an existing takeaway is not a new contribution.

## Reading mode

- Live talk: concise visible argument; speaker support supplies examples and transitions.
- Close read: the argument, decision-critical evidence, and qualifications stand without the presenter.
- Dual use: the complete central argument and material limitations stay visible; speech adds context.

Necessary qualifications remain close to the claim in every mode. Material needed to avoid misleading a reader cannot disappear into notes.

## Plan before filling pages

For a substantial deck, make a short brief and page roster with audience questions, primary propositions, evidence needs, and sequence. Allocate required material to visible copy, speaker support, appendix, or a reasoned exclusion. This provides coverage without making every source detail its own slide.

Use one primary owner for a proposition. Repetition is useful when it performs a different job, such as orientation, a new comparison, or an earned conclusion. Explain that job when duplication is disputed.

## A useful page record

Default to the fields needed for the current writing task:

- page label and audience question;
- primary proposition and its contribution to the sequence;
- headline and visible copy;
- speaker support when requested or required by reading mode;
- sources and material qualifiers;
- transition and production intent when another owner needs them.

Keep a page's copy, narration, and production advice adjacent in labeled subsections when the user needs a page-by-page handoff. Their purposes stay separate even when colocated. Preserve a supplied manuscript structure.

### Optional depth profiles

Use these only when planning a large or reusable handoff:

- Light: orientation, cover, divider, or a simple bounded page; record its job, copy, and transition. Source details apply only to actual claims.
- Standard: substantive page; include evidence, audience before/after, necessary speaker support, and retained qualifiers.
- Full: central quantitative or disputed claim, decision page, or complex handoff; add relevant objections, source limits, dependencies, and production needs.

Profile depth follows actual risk and use. It is not a per-page paperwork quota. Human approval is recorded when it matters to the next step, not inferred from selecting a profile.

## Write the page

When revising, locate the defect in sequence, framing, or supporting prose. If the body already supports the intended point, first judge whether changing the title or paragraph leads is enough. Preserve mature passages; extend the rewrite where the new framing lacks support, a necessary understanding is missing, or a material qualification has become unclear.

Give each level its own job:

- Page title: orient the audience to the subject, question, or supported takeaway. A professional concept can be the clearest label when the body explains it concretely.
- Paragraph lead: frame the point of the passage and its relevance to this audience.
- Body: supply the explanation, examples, evidence, and conditions that earn the point.
- Section: group pages into a distinct advance in the argument and prepare the next advance.
- Summary: connect what the audience has gained, the author's established position, and any warranted next action. Report production effort only when it is relevant evidence for this audience.

For an argument or evidence page, make the supported takeaway clear and supply the evidence that earns it. For a cover, divider, agenda, question, exercise, or reveal, preserve its actual job, including an intentional question whose answer belongs later. Choose a scene, comparison, mechanism, evidence, or decision tradeoff according to that job. Explain relationships in the body so a concise heading can carry a useful abstraction.

For example, a method page can use “Preventive maintenance” above a concrete comparison of planned inspections and breakdown repairs. Expanding that title into a long question adds little if the body already makes the distinction clear.

Speaker support adds context, interpretation within evidence bounds, examples, and transitions. It should not repeat the screen or introduce unsupported facts. Author reasoning and source-management notes stay out of audience copy.

## Capacity

Judge information demand using the primary claim, visible wording, evidence, qualifiers, hierarchy, and speaker dependence. An actual template or native example can supply layout capacity. Without one, say that fit remains unverified and continue producing a content candidate.

For formal capacity feedback, identify the page, reading mode, content demand, known template limits, and the effect on reading. Follow [Findings and repair scope](quality-gates-and-profiles.md#findings-and-repair-scope): propose a split, reallocation, or simplification when the requested scope includes solutions; a findings-only review explains the problem and desired reading result. Preserve necessary qualifications and evidence. Do not claim a page fits merely because its word count is low.

## Continuity and appendices

Check that each page's conclusion gives the audience what the next page assumes. Repair the smallest missing bridge. In close-read or dual-use decks, an essential bridge cannot be speech-only.

Place decision-driving evidence beside the claim or give a usable nearby pointer. An appendix can supply depth; each item should support a body question or a stated diligence need. Body pointers must resolve. Agenda, summary, and close should match the actual argument and must not strengthen it.

When splitting, merging, moving, or deleting pages, follow affected claims, qualifiers, narration, cross-references, and transitions. Use a small mapping when needed so material is not lost; a formal dependency graph is optional for genuinely complex cases.

## Multiple decks and incomplete inputs

For a series, define each deck's audience job and exclusive topics. Shared facts may recur for different reader purposes. A visual reference never transfers content ownership.

If a requested narration depends on unseen page copy, read the named source if available. Otherwise request only the missing page responsibility, visible text, or facts. Keep supplied page responsibilities and reading mode intact and continue independently supported units.

## Example: an evidence boundary changes the sequence

Suppose a brief reports fewer errors after a process change, but only one shift tried it and there was no comparison group. A sequence that declares “proven across the business” and then asks for full rollout overstates the evidence. A supported sequence can show the bounded observation, explain the proposed mechanism as a hypothesis, and present the brief’s authorized next test or decision. The quantitative limit stays next to the number in screen copy; it does not disappear into speech.

For a training deck, an opening question can legitimately precede its answer. Check whether the learner can attempt the question with what they already know; reveal the explanation where it teaches, rather than converting every title into a conclusion.
