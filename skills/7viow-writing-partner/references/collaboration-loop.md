# Develop Voice Through Feedback

Read for mixed examples, the author's own revisions, repeated feedback, uncertain preferences, or a transition from exploration to an agreed draft.

## Locate the uncertainty

Start with the current text and known decisions. Supplied material and a clear goal are enough to begin; use them for the next revision or comparison. A missing reader or purpose may change the entire piece; a vague “too stiff” usually needs a small language experiment. Ask only for the detail that changes the next useful unit. A reversible stylistic assumption can be stated briefly and tried.

Choose a sample for voice, an outline for order, a paragraph for argument, and the requested complete piece when the direction is settled. If the user asks only to compare, stop after the comparison and recommendation. If the user asks you to choose and write, choose within the brief and finish that scope.

## Read examples and author edits

Compare the original with what the author actually deletes, keeps, reorders, emphasizes, or rewrites. Distinguish what each change establishes:

- Language preference: wording, rhythm, vocabulary, emotional temperature, or reader distance.
- Information weight: which fact leads, receives space, or becomes supporting detail.
- Communication stance: whose purpose the passage serves and what it asks the reader to understand or do.
- Concept change: a term now names a different object or covers a different scope; carry that change into the related body text.
- Business decision: a new fact, service, eligibility condition, or commitment comes from the user's decision and supporting material, not from stylistic freedom.

Infer only what the edit supports. Deletion may remove repetition rather than reject the underlying idea; retained body text is evidence of what can remain. A title or opening can give existing material a new purpose without requiring a rewrite of every paragraph. Keep independent, accepted dimensions settled.

“Use A's rhythm and B's order” transfers those traits, not A's biography or B's metrics. A short sentence may feel direct because its action is clear; reproducing its length alone does not reproduce its voice. Point to the observable difference rather than calling alternatives “premium” or “more human.”

Use supplied facts for factual samples. For an unavailable biography, request the smallest relevant real event rather than inventing a convincing career. Explicit fictional briefs permit invention inside their declared setting.

Let supported product benefits, commercial goals, and the originator's purpose carry their intended force. Place conditions where they help the reader judge the claim. A clear interest or persuasive stance can coexist with accurate facts.

### Example: a new emphasis with the same body

Original title and opening:

> Inventory tools for small shops
>
> The app includes stock tracking and alerts.

Author's replacement:

> Know what to restock next
>
> We built this to make each restocking decision easier for shop owners.

Body retained in both versions:

> Shop owners set a minimum stock level for each item. The dashboard shows items below that level and lets them export a restocking list.

The edit moves the emphasis from a feature list to the shop owner's task and the maker's purpose. Keep the useful feature explanation. A usable anchor is “Know what to restock next”: lead with the reader's task when the passage supports it.

By contrast, changing “Contact us for support” to “We answer every support request within two hours” adds a service deadline. Treat it as a new business decision requiring the user's stated commitment, rather than carrying it over as a voice trait.

## Turn feedback into a revision

Separate what the feedback preserves, rejects, and leaves open:

- “Keep the opening; make the middle warmer” preserves the opening and changes the middle's relationship to the reader.
- “Keep the order; it still sounds like a brochure” calls for a language revision within the accepted order.
- “Use the new ending” replaces the identified earlier ending when its referent is clear.
- “Shorter, but keep the conditions” calls for tighter wording, not removal of the conditions.

For an unclear reaction, show a small revision that tests one plausible interpretation and explain that change in plain language. When comparing candidates, provide actual sentences or passages that differ in the open dimension so the author can judge their effect. Ask a focused question only when materially different interpretations cannot be resolved from the text.

Preserve a rejected direction and its reason. A new title does not make the same rejected reveal acceptable. If a new source changes a claim, reopen that claim and affected dependencies; retain independent voice and wording decisions.

### Continue a reviewed candidate set

When the user reviews by number, retain stable identifiers and the wording of each accepted candidate. Distinguish accepted text, requested revisions, newly proposed text awaiting feedback, and deleted candidates. A generated replacement remains a proposal; “continue” authorizes more work without approving every earlier candidate. Preserve a deleted identifier as a gap unless the user asks for renumbering.

Apply feedback to its actual scope. “Keep this and expand to parents” preserves the line while adding a new audience; “too intimate” changes the relationship implied, not merely the warmth of its adjectives. A local vocabulary objection guides that audience and piece rather than establishing a language-wide ban.

If the user asks to see only changes, show additions, replacements, and deletions while maintaining the full set in the agreed working artifact. Keep accepted text untouched there. When asked for the complete set, deliver it. Use an existing file or compact working notes as needed; a short isolated edit needs no candidate register.

## Build a longer piece

Keep a few usable anchors: an accepted sentence or short passage beside the trait it demonstrates and where that trait applies. Treat a one-off word choice as local unless explicit feedback or repeated edits establish a wider preference. Different sections can perform different jobs while sharing stance, vocabulary, and reader distance. Carry required facts and qualifications through expansion and compression.

When feedback concerns one section, test the repair there before propagating it to similar passages. Propagate the changed dimension and its necessary dependencies. An approved direction remains actionable: integrate or complete the requested draft under the current authorization.

For an extended collaboration, maintain lightweight notes only while they help: current version or excerpt, anchor traits, accepted choices, rejected traits, and a consequential unresolved issue. Keep these out of finished copy unless the user or next owner needs them.

## Finish the round

Finish when the actual passage expresses the requested change, carries any changed concept through its affected text, and remains consistent with the supported facts and accepted anchors. A no-change judgment is useful when another edit would only exchange equally valid wording.

Limited review attention affects what to show first; the writing instruction determines what may be completed. A request for one paragraph of feedback does not imply permission to rewrite the rest, and a request to complete a draft does not become a new permission gate at every paragraph.

Deliver the requested writing or concrete choice. Continue where the direction is already authorized; name a real missing fact or consequential choice when it prevents further progress.
